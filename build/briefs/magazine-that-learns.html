A magazine that learns

A strategic learning redesign for The American Bahá’í

Recast as a system of engaging, newsletter-oriented story products and well-placed invitations, The American Bahá’í magazine can work as a learning platform, research tool, and content engine that supplies resources and insight to support the needs of many different content projects and audiences.

01The digital edition

The proposed digital edition rebuilds each issue story by story. Working from the final magazine proof, it gives every story an opening drawn from its print design (a broadside for Louis Gregory, a brush-stroke title for New Yorkers, a pen-traced script for two artists, a scholarly footnote for fifty years of Bahá’í studies, postmarks on a quilt for communities across America) to lead a richly reimagined, fully accessible, mobile-first layout. It then extends each story with the digital canvas: dynamic timelines, infographic explanations, marginalia, expanded photography sections, animated art, embedded video, audio versions and interactive maps. Stories go out as each is ready, by newsletter at the pace a reader chooses, and finally gather as the issue.

02From readers to collaborators

TAB’s digital edition widens readership from its print base, placing curated, feature stories online, designed mobile first and in audio, reaching readers print could not: those conducting searches with spiritual or service-oriented terms, younger Bahá’ís who read on their phones, seekers who arrive through share links, and readers with impaired vision or other disabilities. Each comes in by a route fitted to them: the landing page, the newsletter, the curated public site, or sign-in with a Bahá’í ID to the members’ full edition.

The newsletter carries the season between issues: each subscriber chooses one story as it is published, a few at a time, or the full issue, so a reader can take a little without committing to everything, the season’s theme, statement or questions stay in view between visits, and every email leads to a story that ends in Send a story. Each issue then puts questions and themes to the community, and readers help plan each season through reader surveys, open questions and theme notices. Answering from their local context, they become sources of leads and of what is being learned; then contributors of stories, photographs and field notes; and, for some, regular writers, photographers and filmmakers working with the editors. Everyone who sends something hears back within a promised time.

03What the audience shares

What the audience shares is banked as lead reports and in media repositories, each item recorded with consent, credit and information for reuse. From that bank the magazine draws fresh stories and story ingredients for many projects: features from the grassroots, told in fresher styles and from more places; microstories for social media and newsletters; a photo, b-roll and stock library of recent, real activity; briefs for what the content planners might consider next; notes to share in learning desk meetings; and raw assets other teams can draw on for their projects and audiences. Each season also runs as a small test, and the magazine tracks what draws readers inward to deeper engagement.

04The 2026 Audience Report

The model treats its readers and visitors as the 2026 Audience Report describes its subject: people in motion, moving between orientations as their needs change. The report found that seekers first look for real gatherings and real people, and that engaged readers want practical guidance and genuine voices; the new TAB model supplies both from the grassroots, in stories and footage made by the people who were there. It urges empowering individual Bahá’ís, especially youth, to create authentic content, and the model moves readers toward regular work with the editors. The report’s coda observes that the quality of the channels we build determines what our audience can tell us. The magazine is built as such a feedback channel, so what readers send each season will inform and enrich future cycles, the report’s among them.

Each season can also run small tests, e.g. whether a question (“What helps a class grow where you live?”) draws more answers than a plain invitation to send a story. Heatmaps show where reading stops, whether the call at the close of a story is reached, and what readers especially engage with. Results are written up as Learning notes at the season’s end and shared where relevant.
